Types of phishing

The delivery changes, the trick doesn't: make someone act before they think.

Email phishing

Sent to thousands at once

The classic. A message pretending to be a bank, a delivery firm or a software provider, built to get you onto a fake login page.

"Your parcel could not be delivered. Confirm your address within 24 hours."From: delivery@parcel-notice-center.info

Look for: a generic greeting, a sender domain that doesn't match the company, and a link that goes somewhere else.

Spear phishing

Written for one person

The attacker has done homework. They know your name, your manager, maybe the project you're on, which makes the message feel ordinary. It is the hardest type to spot by sight alone.

"Hi Sam, can you look at the attached budget for the Harlow project before Dan's meeting?"

Look for: unexpected attachments, even from familiar names, and requests that skip your normal process.

Smishing

Phishing by text message

Texts feel more personal and people read nearly all of them. Short links hide the real destination, and a phone screen gives you little room to inspect anything.

"Toll unpaid. Pay now to avoid a fine: tollpay-secure.top/inv"

Look for: unexpected fees, links from unknown numbers, and anything that pressures you to pay quickly.

Vishing

Phishing by phone call

A caller claims to be from your bank, IT desk or the tax office. Voices can be spoofed on caller ID, and attackers can sound calm and rehearsed. Some now use cloned voices.

"This is IT. I'm sending you a code, please read it back so I can lock your account."

Look for: requests for one-time codes or passwords. Real support teams never need those from you.

Fake websites

Pharming and lookalike pages

A page copied pixel for pixel from a real login screen. Anything you type goes straight to the attacker. Lookalike domains swap letters, for example a zero for an o.

Looks like your bank's login pagehttps://secure-bank0fexample.com/login

Look for: the actual domain, which is the part just before the first single slash. Better still, use a bookmark.

CEO fraud

Also called business email compromise

Someone poses as a senior person and asks a staff member to pay an invoice, buy gift cards or change bank details, usually while asking for secrecy.

"I'm in a meeting and can't call. Need this paid today, please keep it between us."

Look for: secrecy, urgency and a change from how payments normally work. Confirm using a number you already have.

The psychology behind it

Attackers rarely need clever code. They lean on a handful of feelings that make people skip the checking step.

Urgency

"Within 24 hours." A deadline leaves no time to ask anyone.

Authority

The boss, the bank, the police. People obey titles by habit.

Fear

Your account is locked, you owe money, there's a warrant.

Reward

A refund, a prize, a bonus. Free things switch off suspicion.

Curiosity

"See who viewed your profile." Some clicks are just nosiness.

Trust

A name you know, a logo you've seen a hundred times.
