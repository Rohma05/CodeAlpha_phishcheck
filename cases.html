Real cases

None of these victims were careless amateurs. That's the point.

2016

The Podesta emails

John Podesta, chair of a US presidential campaign, received what looked like a Google security alert telling him to change his password. A staffer who checked it with IT got an answer that was later said to be a typo: he meant to type "illegitimate" and wrote "legitimate". Podesta clicked, and thousands of emails were later published.

Type
Spear phishing with a shortened link
Lesson
A short link hides its destination. Go to the account page yourself instead of following the alert.
2011

RSA SecurID

RSA, a security company, was breached after two small groups of employees received emails titled "2011 Recruitment plan". An Excel attachment held an exploit for a flaw in Flash. Data connected to its SecurID authentication products was stolen, and the company later spent heavily on replacements.

Type
Spear phishing with a booby-trapped attachment
Lesson
Even a security firm can be fooled by a dull, believable subject line.
2020

The Twitter hack

Attackers phoned Twitter employees pretending to be internal IT, then directed them to a fake login page. With the stolen access they took over accounts of well-known people and posted a Bitcoin scam. The main suspect was a teenager.

Type
Vishing plus a fake login site
Lesson
A confident voice isn't proof. Hang up and call back on a number you trust.
2013-15

The Google and Facebook invoices

A Lithuanian man, Evaldas Rimasauskas, set up a company with the same name as a real hardware supplier and sent invoices to Google and Facebook. Their accounts teams paid. Over two years, the total passed 100 million US dollars. He was later caught, pleaded guilty and was sentenced to prison.

Type
Business email compromise using fake invoices
Lesson
Check changes to payee details with a known contact, however routine the invoice looks.
